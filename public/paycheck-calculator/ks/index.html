How Kansas taxes wages

Kansas uses progressive brackets, so only the income inside each band is taxed at that band's rate. On a $70,300 salary the state tax comes to $3,634, an effective 5.2% — lower than the top bracket you reach.

Kansas taxes its first dollar at 5.2%; the band containing $70,300 starts at $0 and charges 5.6%. Going from $70,300 to $87,875 adds $981 of state tax.

What is specific to Kansas here

Your marginal rate — what you lose on the next dollar earned — is 35.2% in Kansas, against 29.6% in Texas. That is the number that matters for overtime, a raise or a second job.

What is different about Kansas

  • Some cities and counties levy their own payroll or wage taxes in Kansas. Those are local, not state, and excluded here.
  • 2 income bands. The top band starts at $23,000 of taxable income and charges 5.6%.
  • Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages. At $70,300 you are below that line, so the full 6.2% applies to every paycheck.

Where $70,300 lands in Kansas

Kansas households have a median income of $70,333, ranking 37 of 51 jurisdictions against a national median of $77,719. A gross salary of $70,300 is 100% of the Kansas median and 90% of the national one — $33 below the state midpoint.

By percentile, that puts a single earner in the 20th-40th percentile of Kansas households. Read this as a rough marker, not an exact position: the percentiles count whole households, which often have two earners, while this salary is one person's gross.

Where Kansas income sits in the tax structure

What this page does not model

Single filer, standard deduction, no 401(k), no health premium, no dependents, no local or city income tax, and state credits and personal exemptions are excluded — which means the state figure is a slight overstatement. Self-employment would double the FICA share.
