How Kentucky taxes wages

Kentucky applies a flat 3.5% rate to taxable wages after a $3,360 standard deduction. A flat rate means the percentage does not rise with income, but the dollar amount does: at $50,000 you pay about $1,632, at $100,000 about $3,382.

Kentucky is one of 15 states with a single wage-tax rate. Its first $3,360 of gross wages is untaxed, which means the $61,100 salary pays state tax on $57,740.

Federal tax applies everywhere

Whatever the state does, the federal side is identical. On $61,100 a single filer pays $5,152 of federal income tax after the $16,100 standard deduction, plus $4,674 in Social Security and Medicare.

What is specific to Kentucky here

Your marginal rate — what you lose on the next dollar earned — is 23.2% in Kentucky, against 19.7% in Texas. That is the number that matters for overtime, a raise or a second job.

What is different about Kentucky

  • Local income taxes add about 0.93% of AGI on average in Kentucky, on top of the state rate. County and city taxes are excluded from the calculator above.
  • Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages. At $61,100 you are below that line, so the full 6.2% applies to every paycheck.

Where $61,100 lands in Kentucky

Kentucky households have a median income of $61,118, ranking 47 of 51 jurisdictions against a national median of $77,719. A gross salary of $61,100 is 100% of the Kentucky median and 79% of the national one — $18 below the state midpoint.

By percentile, that puts a single earner in the 20th-40th percentile of Kentucky households. Read this as a rough marker, not an exact position: the percentiles count whole households, which often have two earners, while this salary is one person's gross.

Where Kentucky income sits in the tax structure

Your first $16,100 of gross pay is covered by the federal standard deduction, so federal income tax starts above that line. Kentucky adds its own $3,360 deduction before state tax starts.

What this page does not model

Single filer, standard deduction, no 401(k), no health premium, no dependents, no local or city income tax, and state credits and personal exemptions are excluded — which means the state figure is a slight overstatement. Self-employment would double the FICA share.
