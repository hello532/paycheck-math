How Louisiana taxes wages

Louisiana applies a flat 3.0% rate to taxable wages after a $12,875 standard deduction. A flat rate means the percentage does not rise with income, but the dollar amount does: at $50,000 you pay about $1,114, at $100,000 about $2,614.

Louisiana is one of 15 states with a single wage-tax rate. Its first $12,875 of gross wages is untaxed, which means the $58,200 salary pays state tax on $45,325.

Federal tax applies everywhere

Whatever the state does, the federal side is identical. On $58,200 a single filer pays $4,804 of federal income tax after the $16,100 standard deduction, plus $4,452 in Social Security and Medicare.

What is specific to Louisiana here

Your marginal rate — what you lose on the next dollar earned — is 22.7% in Louisiana, against 19.7% in Texas. That is the number that matters for overtime, a raise or a second job.

What is different about Louisiana

  • Federal income tax is deductible on the state return. Louisiana lets filers subtract some or all federal tax paid from state taxable income, which lowers the real state bill below what the flat table shows.
  • Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages. At $58,200 you are below that line, so the full 6.2% applies to every paycheck.

Where $58,200 lands in Louisiana

Louisiana households have a median income of $58,229, ranking 49 of 51 jurisdictions against a national median of $77,719. A gross salary of $58,200 is 100% of the Louisiana median and 75% of the national one — $29 below the state midpoint.

By percentile, that puts a single earner in the 20th-40th percentile of Louisiana households. Read this as a rough marker, not an exact position: the percentiles count whole households, which often have two earners, while this salary is one person's gross.

Where Louisiana income sits in the tax structure

Your first $16,100 of gross pay is covered by the federal standard deduction, so federal income tax starts above that line. Louisiana adds its own $12,875 deduction before state tax starts.

What this page does not model

Single filer, standard deduction, no 401(k), no health premium, no dependents, no local or city income tax, and state credits and personal exemptions are excluded — which means the state figure is a slight overstatement. Self-employment would double the FICA share.
