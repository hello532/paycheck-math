How Colorado taxes wages

Colorado applies a flat 4.4% rate to taxable wages after a $16,100 standard deduction. A flat rate means the percentage does not rise with income, but the dollar amount does: at $50,000 you pay about $1,492, at $100,000 about $3,692.

Colorado is one of 14 states with a single wage-tax rate. Its first $16,100 of gross wages is untaxed, which means the $92,900 salary pays state tax on $76,800.

Federal tax applies everywhere

Whatever the state does, the federal side is identical. On $92,900 a single filer pays $11,608 of federal income tax after the $16,100 standard deduction, plus $7,107 in Social Security and Medicare.

What is specific to Colorado here

Your marginal rate — what you lose on the next dollar earned — is 34.1% in Colorado, against 29.6% in Texas. That is the number that matters for overtime, a raise or a second job.

What is different about Colorado

  • Some cities and counties levy their own payroll or wage taxes in Colorado. Those are local, not state, and excluded here.
  • The state income starting point includes the federal standard deduction. Colorado builds its state taxable income on top of the federal figure, so the $16,100 federal deduction is already reflected.
  • Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages. At $92,900 you are below that line, so the full 6.2% applies to every paycheck.

Where $92,900 lands in Colorado

Colorado households have a median income of $92,911, ranking 10 of 51 jurisdictions against a national median of $77,719. A gross salary of $92,900 is 100% of the Colorado median and 120% of the national one — $11 below the state midpoint.

By percentile, that puts a single earner in the 20th-40th percentile of Colorado households. Read this as a rough marker, not an exact position: the percentiles count whole households, which often have two earners, while this salary is one person's gross.

What this page does not model

Single filer, standard deduction, no 401(k), no health premium, no dependents, no local or city income tax, and state credits and personal exemptions are excluded — which means the state figure is a slight overstatement. Self-employment would double the FICA share.
