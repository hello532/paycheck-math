How Indiana taxes wages

Indiana applies a flat 2.9% rate to taxable wages. A flat rate means the percentage does not rise with income, but the dollar amount does: at $50,000 you pay about $1,475, at $100,000 about $2,950.

Indiana is one of 14 states with a single wage-tax rate. Its first $0 of gross wages is untaxed (no standard deduction applies, so tax runs from the first dollar), which means the $69,500 salary pays state tax on $69,500.

Federal tax applies everywhere

Whatever the state does, the federal side is identical. On $69,500 a single filer pays $6,460 of federal income tax after the $16,100 standard deduction, plus $5,317 in Social Security and Medicare.

What is specific to Indiana here

Your marginal rate — what you lose on the next dollar earned — is 32.6% in Indiana, against 29.6% in Texas. That is the number that matters for overtime, a raise or a second job.

What is different about Indiana

  • Local income taxes add about 0.35% of AGI on average in Indiana, on top of the state rate. County and city taxes are excluded from the calculator above.
  • Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages. At $69,500 you are below that line, so the full 6.2% applies to every paycheck.

Where $69,500 lands in Indiana

Indiana households have a median income of $69,477, ranking 38 of 51 jurisdictions against a national median of $77,719. A gross salary of $69,500 is 100% of the Indiana median and 89% of the national one — $23 above it.

By percentile, that puts a single earner in the 40th-60th percentile of Indiana households. Read this as a rough marker, not an exact position: the percentiles count whole households, which often have two earners, while this salary is one person's gross.

Where Indiana income sits in the tax structure

Your first $16,100 of gross pay is covered by the federal standard deduction, so federal income tax starts above that line. Indiana has no standard deduction for this calculation, so state tax applies from the first dollar.

What this page does not model

Single filer, standard deduction, no 401(k), no health premium, no dependents, no local or city income tax, and state credits and personal exemptions are excluded — which means the state figure is a slight overstatement. Self-employment would double the FICA share.
