How Minnesota taxes wages

Minnesota uses progressive brackets, so only the income inside each band is taxed at that band's rate. On a $85,100 salary the state tax comes to $4,263, an effective 5.0% — lower than the top bracket you reach.

Minnesota taxes its first dollar at 5.3%; the band containing $85,100 starts at $0 and charges 6.8%. Going from $85,100 to $106,375 adds $1,447 of state tax.

Federal tax applies everywhere

What is specific to Minnesota here

Your marginal rate — what you lose on the next dollar earned — is 36.5% in Minnesota, against 29.6% in Texas. That is the number that matters for overtime, a raise or a second job.

What is different about Minnesota

  • 4 income bands. The top band starts at $203,150 of taxable income and charges 9.8%.
  • Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages. At $85,100 you are below that line, so the full 6.2% applies to every paycheck.

Where $85,100 lands in Minnesota

Minnesota households have a median income of $85,086, ranking 14 of 51 jurisdictions against a national median of $77,719. A gross salary of $85,100 is 100% of the Minnesota median and 109% of the national one — $14 above it.

By percentile, that puts a single earner in the 40th-60th percentile of Minnesota households. Read this as a rough marker, not an exact position: the percentiles count whole households, which often have two earners, while this salary is one person's gross.

Where Minnesota income sits in the tax structure

Your first $16,100 of gross pay is covered by the federal standard deduction, so federal income tax starts above that line. Minnesota adds its own $15,300 deduction before state tax starts.

What this page does not model

Single filer, standard deduction, no 401(k), no health premium, no dependents, no local or city income tax, and state credits and personal exemptions are excluded — which means the state figure is a slight overstatement. Self-employment would double the FICA share.
