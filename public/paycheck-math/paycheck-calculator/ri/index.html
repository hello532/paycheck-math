How Rhode Island taxes wages

Rhode Island uses progressive brackets, so only the income inside each band is taxed at that band's rate. On a $85,000 salary the state tax comes to $2,768, an effective 3.3% — lower than the top bracket you reach.

Rhode Island taxes its first dollar at 3.8%; the band containing $85,000 starts at $0 and charges 3.8%. Going from $85,000 to $106,250 adds $927 of state tax.

Federal tax applies everywhere

Whatever the state does, the federal side is identical. On $85,000 a single filer pays $9,870 of federal income tax after the $16,100 standard deduction, plus $6,503 in Social Security and Medicare.

What is specific to Rhode Island here

Your marginal rate — what you lose on the next dollar earned — is 33.4% in Rhode Island, against 29.6% in Texas. That is the number that matters for overtime, a raise or a second job.

What is different about Rhode Island

  • 3 income bands. The top band starts at $186,450 of taxable income and charges 6.0%.
  • Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages. At $85,000 you are below that line, so the full 6.2% applies to every paycheck.

Where $85,000 lands in Rhode Island

Rhode Island households have a median income of $84,972, ranking 15 of 51 jurisdictions against a national median of $77,719. A gross salary of $85,000 is 100% of the Rhode Island median and 109% of the national one — $28 above it.

By percentile, that puts a single earner in the 40th-60th percentile of Rhode Island households. Read this as a rough marker, not an exact position: the percentiles count whole households, which often have two earners, while this salary is one person's gross.

Where Rhode Island income sits in the tax structure

Your first $16,100 of gross pay is covered by the federal standard deduction, so federal income tax starts above that line. Rhode Island adds its own $11,200 deduction before state tax starts.

What this page does not model

Single filer, standard deduction, no 401(k), no health premium, no dependents, no local or city income tax, and state credits and personal exemptions are excluded — which means the state figure is a slight overstatement. Self-employment would double the FICA share.
