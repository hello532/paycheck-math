How Wisconsin taxes wages

Wisconsin uses progressive brackets, so only the income inside each band is taxed at that band's rate. On a $74,600 salary the state tax comes to $2,610, an effective 3.5% — lower than the top bracket you reach.

Wisconsin taxes its first dollar at 3.5%; the band containing $74,600 starts at $0 and charges 5.3%. Going from $74,600 to $93,250 adds $988 of state tax.

What is specific to Wisconsin here

Your marginal rate — what you lose on the next dollar earned — is 35.0% in Wisconsin, against 29.6% in Texas. That is the number that matters for overtime, a raise or a second job.

What is different about Wisconsin

  • 4 income bands. The top band starts at $332,720 of taxable income and charges 7.6%.
  • Social Security stops at $184,500 of wages. At $74,600 you are below that line, so the full 6.2% applies to every paycheck.

Where $74,600 lands in Wisconsin

Wisconsin households have a median income of $74,631, ranking 27 of 51 jurisdictions against a national median of $77,719. A gross salary of $74,600 is 100% of the Wisconsin median and 96% of the national one — $31 below the state midpoint.

By percentile, that puts a single earner in the 20th-40th percentile of Wisconsin households. Read this as a rough marker, not an exact position: the percentiles count whole households, which often have two earners, while this salary is one person's gross.

Where Wisconsin income sits in the tax structure

Your first $16,100 of gross pay is covered by the federal standard deduction, so federal income tax starts above that line. Wisconsin adds its own $13,960 deduction before state tax starts.

What this page does not model

Single filer, standard deduction, no 401(k), no health premium, no dependents, no local or city income tax, and state credits and personal exemptions are excluded — which means the state figure is a slight overstatement. Self-employment would double the FICA share.
